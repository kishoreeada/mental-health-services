What a boundary can sound like.

Finding language for your time, capacity, and preferences without writing a defence of your worth.

A clear statement of capacity

A boundary can begin as an ordinary statement about what you can offer: 'I can help on Friday, but not tonight.' It need not explain every detail of your day or persuade everyone that your reason is important enough.

The wording will depend on the relationship and the situation. A work request, a family arrangement, and a friendship may each need a different kind of conversation.

A few examples to adapt

'I need some time to think before I commit.' 'I am available for half an hour.' 'I would rather keep that private.' These are examples of language, not scripts that will fit every circumstance or guarantee a particular response.

You can be warm and specific at the same time. If you genuinely want to offer an alternative, you can. If you do not have an alternative, you do not need to invent one simply to make the sentence feel complete.

Context still matters

Other people's reactions, power differences, and your practical options matter. A short article cannot determine what is safe or possible in your circumstances. You do not owe someone a confrontation, particularly when you are worried about how they might respond.

As a private reflection, notice the difference between a yes you mean and a yes you feel unable to question. You might choose to talk that difference through with someone you trust or with a qualified professional before deciding what to do next.

A question to take with you

What can I genuinely offer, and what am I not available for?

You do not have to answer it today.
